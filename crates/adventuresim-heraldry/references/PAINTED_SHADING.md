# Painted shading in heraldic objects

This reference study concerns the evidence relevant to a German setting in
1544. It distinguishes surviving decorated objects from pictures of objects,
and evidence that a technique existed from evidence of its prevalence. Sources
were checked on 15 September 2026. Earlier English and Italian material is
comparative evidence, not a German workshop specification.

**Finding:** some late medieval and Renaissance artists deliberately painted
shading on physical heraldic objects. The hypothesis that all such shading
belongs only to manuscript representations is untenable. However, a modeled
charge in an armorial does not establish how its counterpart on a particular
shield was manufactured. Painted illusion, actual relief, and flat drawing
must remain distinct possibilities.

The cases below establish existence, not a representative census. They do not
establish the usual finish of an ordinary German fighting shield in 1544, or
authenticate the particular highlights in our modern lion SVG.

The distinctions used in assessing the evidence are:

| Feature | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contour or anatomical line | A drawn division, edge, or detail. | A directional shadow or physical groove. |
| Painted tonal modeling | Light and dark paint used to suggest form. | Actual sculptural relief. |
| Painted cast shadow | An illusion of one form interrupting light. | A shadow that should move with the scene light. |
| Real relief, leaf, or embroidery | A physical structure capable of catching light. | The absence of additional painted modeling. |
| Shading in an illustration | How the illustrator presented the subject. | A fabrication specification for the represented object. |

For surviving objects, the relevant questions are the support, paint sequence,
date of the relevant layer, later restoration, and intended use. For pictures,
the additional question is whether the artist depicts paint, sculpture,
metalwork, a curved shield, or an imagined living creature.

**The strongest German case is the Ketzel-Koler shield, Met 25.26.5.**
The museum dates the targe to c. 1450–1500. Two small armorial shields show the
Ketzel monkey holding a ball and the Koler ring. The current catalogue suggests
that these arms were added after Lucas Ketzel's death in 1485 for memorial
display. They are not securely the shield's original fighting decoration.
[Met object catalogue](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/27019).

Faltermeier and Meyer's technical examination identifies painted shadows to
the lower right of both charges in the third paint campaign. The underlying
raised letters also have painted light and dark edges. These observations
come from examination of paint layers, not merely interpretation of a museum
photograph. The same report distinguishes later, nonmedieval cast shadows on
another shield, 25.26.1; an old support does not date every visible brushstroke.
[Conservation report, pp. 53–55, 58–59](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Appendix_Notes_on_the_Restoration_of_the_Behaim_Shields_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_30_1995.pdf).

Helmut Nickel places the striped and lettered campaign shortly before 1485
and the Ketzel-Koler addition shortly afterward. This is historical and
stylistic dating, not a laboratory determination of the year. It supports
late fifteenth-century use in a Nuremberg family context, with the memorial
qualification retained.
[Nickel, *The Seven Shields of Behaim: New Evidence*, p. 42](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Seven_Shields_of_Behaim_New_Evidence_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_30_1995.pdf).

This is decisive against a manuscript-only explanation. It is also a useful
warning about categories: small illusionistic shields painted on a real targe
are simultaneously depictions and physical heraldic decoration. An intention
to suggest volume does not make their painted shadows cease to be paint.

**A near-contemporary textile provides independent evidence.** Marion
Sauter's monument inventory identifies the Uri Juliusbanner as 1512. Its
caption calls the bull painted and explicitly describes finely drawn shading;
it separately identifies the nose ring as raised gold and silver embroidery.
The banner was ceremonial. This is catalogue evidence, not a pigment-by-pigment
authentication of an untouched surface; its restoration correspondence is
listed but was not examined here.
[Sauter, *Die Kunstdenkmäler des Kantons Uri*, III (2017), p. 468, fig. 551; p. 515, note 179](https://www.gsk.ch/sites/default/files/kds-132d-uri-iii-schaechental-und-unteres-reusstal.pdf#page=470).

This case matters because the painted animal is on an actual banner, rather
than inside a manuscript picture of a banner. It supports painted animal
modeling beyond the exceptional arrangement of small shields on a shield.
It does not establish the treatment of every Swiss military flag, much less
every German shield.

**Paint could imitate relief on the physical object itself.** The Met's
Italian shield attributed to Girolamo da Treviso, c. 1535, is a decorated
wooden object with linen, gesso, gold leaf, and polychromy.
[Met 42.50.16](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/24693).
The museum's 1991 publication explicitly interprets its gold-and-black
painting as an imitation of embossed metal. Its subject is classical narrative,
not a heraldic animal, so it is supporting evidence for the decorative
technique rather than a second heraldic charge example.
[Metropolitan Museum Bulletin 49, no. 1 (1991), p. 26](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Arms_and_Armor_Permanent_Collection_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art_Bulletin_v_49_no_1_Summer_1991.pdf).

This resolves the apparent opposition between physical decoration and
illusionism: an artist could paint shadows onto a real shield precisely to
make it resemble relief. A renderer should retain those marks as paint even
though they imitate a material structure the object does not possess.

**There is also real evidence for the alternative explanation.** Actual
heraldic relief existed. The Behaim conservation report finds traces of lost
applied decoration on 25.26.3 and proposes molded, leaf-covered pressed
brocade for its panther and corner ornaments. Its exact former appearance
cannot be recovered from the remaining traces.
[Conservation report, p. 55 and note 3](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Appendix_Notes_on_the_Restoration_of_the_Behaim_Shields_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_30_1995.pdf).
Thus an illustrator could plausibly depict light falling on a raised charge.
That possibility must be evaluated for the particular picture, not applied
as a universal explanation of all modeled heraldry.

The National Gallery's technical catalogue gives a particularly clear example
of representation: Richard II's white-hart jewel in the Wilton Diptych is
painted to resemble white enamel over gold. The apparent volume is part of
depicting jewelry. It would be a mistake to transfer that modeling to a flat
painted badge without further evidence. This late fourteenth-century English
work is a methodological comparison, not local evidence for Germany in 1544.
[National Gallery, Wilton Diptych catalogue, painting technique and fig. 18](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/national-gallery-2024/the-wilton-diptych).

**Workshop instructions support the technique, with limits.** In chapter
CXLIV of Cennino Cennini's *Libro dell'arte*, the discussion of depicting silk
includes dark and light hatching and explicitly extends a color mixture to
panels and shields. The context is representing cloth, not a rule for heraldic
lions. It demonstrates a painter's repertoire, not a universal armorial
prescription. The adjacent chapters also describe layering different tonal
values and painting over metal leaf.
[Cennini, chapters CXLI–CXLVI, Daniel V. Thompson translation](https://noteaccess.com/Texts/Cennini/6TM.htm).

**The conflicting advice often addresses different questions.** The current
Rhineland-Palatinate archive guidance requires common charges in municipal
arms to be flat, without perspective or shading. This is direct evidence of
a present-day design convention. It cannot establish a prohibition observed
by every fifteenth- or sixteenth-century painter.
[Landesarchivverwaltung Rheinland-Pfalz, general heraldic design rules](https://lav.rlp.de/anbieten-und-abgeben/kommunalverwaltungen/kommunale-wappen-und-flaggen).

G. W. Eve's *Heraldry as Art* (1907), p. 29, argues that ordinary working
shields probably used flat colors and allows that manuscript relief might
be an illuminator's elaboration. He distinguishes more elaborate ceremonial
objects. His discussion is relevant to the history of the claim, but is not a
contemporary workshop account or a statistical survey of surviving finishes.
The same book discusses shading and highlights in illumination on p. 164.
[Eve, *Heraldry as Art*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/69298/pg69298-images.html).

The [Eve entry](../REFERENCES.md#historical-interpretation-and-economics)
records the edition. The [gameplay requirements](../GAMEPLAY_INTEGRATION.md)
preserve the distinction between this economic hypothesis and measured
historical prevalence, and between painted modeling and physical relief.

There is consequently no need to choose between an unrestricted naturalistic
style and a claim that all historical heraldry was unshaded. A rule for a
modern emblem, a probable economical fighting finish, an illuminated book,
and a prestigious ceremonial object answer different design needs. The
surviving examples establish possibilities; they do not supply reliable
frequency estimates for those needs.

**Application to the German lion reference.** BSB Cod.icon. 391 is catalogued
as southern Germany (Augsburg?), c. 1530. Fol. 177r contains line hatching;
fol. 286v contains lighter modeling on a black lion. These are direct evidence
for pictorial treatment. Neither page by itself establishes whether the
modeled form represents paint on a physical shield, real relief, or an
illustrator's interpretation.
[BSB manuscript](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00007681),
[fol. 177r](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00007681_00355/full/full/0/default.jpg),
[fol. 286v](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/image/v2/bsb00007681_00574/full/full/0/default.jpg).

Our Tom-L/Rinaldum SVG is a modern adaptation. Its gold palette and precise
highlight shapes are not authenticated sixteenth-century paint instructions.
The [source and modification record](ATTRIBUTION.md) documents that distinction.
The independent object evidence makes optional painted modeling defensible;
it does not turn this specific adaptation into a facsimile.

For the modeler, four choices should remain conceptually separate:

1. Charge drawing: silhouette, anatomy, and interior lines.
2. Painted treatment: flat tincture, drawn detail, or tonal modeling.
3. Physical construction: pigment, leaf, raised decoration, or other relief.
4. Scene illumination: light on the resulting actual surface.

Painted shadows remain fixed to the artwork when the scene light moves.
Physical shading responds to that light. A flat painted lion cannot acquire
modeled muscles from scene illumination alone: the renderer would need either
painted tonal information or corresponding relief. Conversely, a broad light
gradient intended to represent illumination of an entire shield should not
automatically be copied into its paint.

The modeler names this choice **Painted modeling**, with separate shadow and
highlight coverage controls and **Flat** and **Modeled** presets. Drawing and
asymmetry are independent. An unshaded option remains historically defensible;
neither option should claim to be the uniquely authentic finish for 1544.

The material evidence establishes painted shading with high confidence;
the late fifteenth-century attribution of the Ketzel-Koler campaign remains
the published historical interpretation. Prevalence, the manufacture implied
by the particular BSB lion, and the exact historical counterpart of the modern
SVG's tonal layout remain unresolved. Answering those narrower questions
would require a dated corpus with paint-layer studies or a documented link
between a workshop design and a surviving object.
