# Breastplate shape references

These are photographs of surviving objects, viewed in the browser on 2026-09-09.
The observations below describe visible coarse shape, not measured dimensions or
an acceptance verdict for the generated mesh. Compare the front shell separately
from faulds, tassets, gussets, reinforcing plates, straps, and decoration, which
are outside this simple breastplate recipe's scope. Reference photographs and
generated render images remain outside Git.

The Met's [survey of sixteenth-century armor fashion](https://www.metmuseum.org/pt/essays/fashion-in-european-armor-1500-1600)
describes an early rounded breast, a return of the medial ridge and projecting
point from the 1530s, then a progressively lower point towards the late-century
peascod. These are shape families rather than an exact chronology for every
region. The following specific objects anchor visual comparison.

## Rounded, without flutes

- [Met 22741, Greenwich garniture dated 1527](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/22741), accession 19.131.1a-r, t-w, .2a-f, l.
- [Viewed three-quarter photograph](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/api/collection/v1/iiif/22741/60375/main-image).
- Visible target: a broad, softly convex front, smooth chest-to-belly transition,
  short torso ending at a restrained waist, and no long pendant point. The front
  should read as one enclosing volume rather than two anatomical pectoral cups.
- Scope limitation: the full mounted garniture contains reinforcing pieces and
  obscures some upper boundaries. It supports the worn coarse volume, not an
  exact isolated-shell reconstruction or quantitative clearance.

## Mid-century medial ridge

- [Cleveland 1916.1647, Nuremberg, ca. 1550](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1916.1647).
- [Viewed front photograph](https://www.clevelandart.org/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fpiction.clevelandart.org%2Fcma%2Fump.di%3Fe%3DBDF871B322C0BA92B24DF96EF9DAE9704B8E4085EFBFBFFB1025A6CF35A066A2%26s%3D24247294%26se%3D399313166%26v%3D1%26f%3D%5Cd6260%5Cu228062608%5C1916.1647_o5.jpg&w=640&q=75).
- Visible target: an uninterrupted central ridge joining two gently bowed front
  halves; the lower chest projects while the waist returns inward. The front is
  compact, with a shallow central drop at the lower edge and tall curved arm
  openings. It does not have the long lower point of the peascod reference.
- This is a restrained medial-ridged reference. The viewed front photograph alone
  does not establish the depth of a sharply projecting tapul; a stronger tapul
  preset needs a corresponding verified side photograph before shape acceptance.

### Supplemental side profile

- [Allen Collection A-241, attributed to Innsbruck, 1550-60](https://www.allenantiques.com/A-241.html).
- [Viewed side photograph](https://www.allenantiques.com/images/A-241-b-side.jpg).
- This surviving private-collection object supplements Cleveland's frontal
  photograph. Its upper front has a long, nearly straight slope into a localized
  rounded projection in the lower part of the plate, followed by a short inward
  return to the waist. The side image supports that depth distribution; it has
  no wearer and cannot establish numerical chest clearance.
- The attribution is the collection's, with a quoted comparison by Ian Eaves to
  Innsbruck armor at Churburg. It is not a museum accession or a replacement
  reference for the other shape families. Decoration and separate gussets remain
  outside the modeled scope.

## Late-century peascod

- [Worcester 2014.736, Milan, 1575-1600](https://worcester.emuseum.com/objects/59214/peascod-cuirass-torso-armor-for-infantry).
- [Viewed isolated front photograph](https://worcester.emuseum.com/internal/media/dispatcher/50645/preview).
- Visible target: a broad upper shell narrowing into a low central point; the
  bottom rises markedly towards both flanks. A continuous center ridge organizes
  the volume. The lowest point is smoothly rounded rather than a needle or
  free-standing spike. The museum explicitly identifies its point as below the
  waistline. Its articulated gussets and etched bands are separate details.
- [Met 23939, George Clifford's Greenwich garniture, 1586](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/23939), accession 32.130.6a-y, provides the worn silhouette.
- [Viewed three-quarter photograph](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/api/collection/v1/iiif/23939/64763/main-image): a narrow waist at the flanks, a longer front
  descending centrally, and a clear transition to separate flaring hip defenses.

## Rounded with flutes

- [Cleveland 1916.1640.a, Nuremberg(?), ca. 1510-30](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1916.1640.a).
- [Viewed front photograph](https://www.clevelandart.org/_next/image?url=https%3A%2F%2Fpiction.clevelandart.org%2Fcma%2Fump.di%3Fe%3D932C43118495F1B81051E3FD9512527E97C74C36936557AEA27428E480FAD43C%26s%3D24247294%26se%3D399313166%26v%3D1%26f%3D%5Cd6117%5Cu228061170%5C1916.1640.a_o5.jpg&w=640&q=75).
- Visible target: a rounded projecting belly returning to a narrower waist;
  closely spaced vertical channels fan modestly outward towards their upper
  ends. Fluting occupies a bounded band across the front, leaving broad smooth
  upper-chest and waist margins. The underlying shell remains legible beneath
  the corrugation; these are surface channels, not rods laid on top of the plate.
  The attached fauld is outside the front-shell comparison.
- [Met 24807, Nuremberg armor, ca. 1520 and later](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/24807), accession 49.163.1a-s.
- [Viewed three-quarter photograph](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/api/collection/v1/iiif/24807/65320/main-image) supplements the rounded worn volume and finer fluting.
  The museum identifies its breastplate as unusually two-piece and its waist
  plates/tassets as later: do not claim a single-shell recipe reconstructs that
  plate arrangement.

For review, open the relevant photographs beside front, side, and three-quarter
body-included renders. Evaluate shell breadth, chest projection, waist location,
arm clearance, lower-point height, and fluting flow before small details. A
frontal photograph does not certify a side profile; unresolved profile evidence
must remain explicit.
